Antalya

🥙Street Food Guide

Antalya Piyazı (Antalya-Style Bean Salad)

While piyaz is found across Turkey, the Antalya version is a protected geographical indication. Unlike the vinegar-heavy versions elsewhere, Antalya's street-side piyaz features a rich, creamy sauce made from tahini, lemon juice, and garlic.

The Experience: It is typically served as a side to grilled meatballs (köfte) but is hearty enough to be a meal on its own. Look for vendors who top it with hard-boiled eggs, fresh parsley, and a generous drizzle of local olive oil.

Tirmis (Lupini Beans)

If you see a street vendor pushing a small cart with yellow, coin-shaped beans soaking in water, you’ve found Tirmis. This is Antalya’s most idiosyncratic snack.

The Experience: These are boiled lupini beans, seasoned simply with salt and cumin. Locals eat them like sunflower seeds—popping the bean out of its translucent skin. It’s a bitter-salty snack that is highly addictive once you get the hang of it.

Yanık Dondurma (Burnt Ice Cream)

You haven't truly experienced Antalya’s dessert scene until you’ve tried Burnt Ice Cream. Made specifically from the fatty milk of goats fed on wild thyme, the milk is intentionally "scorched" during the boiling process.

The Experience: The flavor is an acquired taste—smoky, creamy, and slightly caramelized. It’s a cooling treat that offers a sophisticated departure from standard vanilla.

Serpme Börek

While börek is a Turkish staple, Antalya’s Serpme Börek is a performance art. Chefs toss and spin the dough until it is paper-thin and translucent before folding it over fillings like minced meat, cheese, or spinach.

The Experience: It is significantly crispier and lighter than the heavy, doughy versions found in other provinces. It’s best enjoyed mid-morning with a glass of strong Turkish tea.

Midye Dolma (Stuffed Mussels)

As a coastal city, Antalya’s night air is often filled with the scent of fresh lemon and spices from Midye Dolma stands. These are orange-fleshed mussels stuffed with herbed rice, currants, and pine nuts.

The Experience: Vendors will stand behind a tray of mussels and open them for you one by one. You squeeze fresh lemon over the shell and use the top shell as a spoon. It is customary to keep eating until you tell the vendor to stop.

Pro Tips for Food Explorers

Follow the School Bells: The best Simit (sesame bread rings) and Tavuk Pilav (chicken and rice) carts are usually found near schools and government buildings during lunch hours.

Seasonal Sips: In the winter, look for vendors selling Boza (a fermented malt drink); in the summer, seek out Taze Sıkılmış Portakal Suyu—Antalya is the citrus capital of Turkey, and the orange juice here is unparalleled.

Hydration: Always look for "Ayran" (a salted yogurt drink) to accompany spicy dishes like Tantuni or Adana Dürüm; it is the traditional way to balance the heat and aid digestion.

Cultural Context: The "Esnaf" Tradition

The quality of street food in Antalya remains high because of the Esnaf (tradesmen) culture. Many of these vendors have occupied the same street corner for decades, relying on a loyal local customer base rather than one-time tourists. This ensures that the ingredients—from the tomatoes grown in nearby greenhouses to the tahini ground in local mills—are of the highest standard.
